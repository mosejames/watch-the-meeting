The Ward 1 Spending Machine: How McKenzie Directed Hundreds of Thousands to Consultants, Friends, and Allies

A Council Watch investigation traces at least $700,000 in contracts and expenditures personally sponsored or directed by Mayor Pro Tem Jamelle McKenzie, most approved via consent agenda with no competitive bidding and, in one case, no identifiable funding source.
January 2, 2024 – June 30, 202649 meetings6 sections

On the evening of June 15, 2026, Mayor Pro Tem Jamelle McKenzie's $90,000 real estate consulting contract collapsed in real time. The item had been placed on the College Park City Council agenda, but when it came up for a vote, nobody on the dais or in staff could identify where the money was supposed to come from. The contract simply died in funding confusion. Minutes earlier, at the same meeting, McKenzie had pushed through two additional $5,000-per-month consultant contracts for Ward 1, and the council had rubber-stamped $1.4 million in consent agenda items in roughly 30 seconds, without a single question.

That June meeting was not an anomaly. It was the latest entry in a pattern stretching back at least two years, in which McKenzie has personally sponsored, directed, or approved contracts and expenditures for Ward 1 totaling in the range of $700,000 to $800,000. The spending spans trail improvements, engineering contracts, a train depot renovation, tourism consulting, video content production, and multiple monthly consulting retainers. Nearly all of it moved through the consent agenda or was approved with minimal discussion and no evidence of competitive bidding on the public record.

Multiple residents and at least one fellow council member have alleged that the beneficiaries of these contracts are McKenzie's personal supporters and friends. McKenzie has defended the practice by arguing that in a city of roughly 15,000 people, personal acquaintance with vendors is unavoidable, and that her personal wealth insulates her from any corrupt motive. The question is whether the spending follows College Park's own procurement ordinance and Georgia's competitive-bidding thresholds, or whether it has created a pipeline of taxpayer dollars flowing to a network with political ties to the most powerful member of the council.

The Ledger: A Running Tally of McKenzie-Linked Spending

The most straightforward way to assess the pattern is to follow the money. Council Watch identified the following McKenzie-sponsored or McKenzie-directed expenditures from public meeting records between October 2024 and June 2026.

On October 10, 2024, a $42,144 Brady Trail improvement project personally sponsored by McKenzie passed on the consent agenda. There was no competitive bid discussion, no ward impact review. It was approved in seconds as part of a consent agenda that buried over $500,000 in spending without a word of discussion.

On May 5, 2025, council approved a $10,000 facade grant to an entity called "Jamelle McKenzie Brothers Realty," which shares a surname with the Mayor Pro Tem. McKenzie seconded the motion. The dossier does not confirm a family relationship, but the shared name drew public attention.

On September 2, 2025, a $40,000 video content contract came before the council. Residents flagged in public comment that the contract had initially been buried in the consent agenda before being moved to the regular agenda under pressure. McKenzie defended the contract on the record.

On September 15, 2025, a $250,000 engineering contract with Prime Engineering for a nature preserve project in Ward 1 was approved. There were no questions, no vendor vetting on the record, and the vote took approximately 30 seconds.

On November 3, 2025, council quietly approved $250,000 for a train depot renovation on the consent agenda, a project publicly championed by McKenzie.

On June 15, 2026, McKenzie approved two more $5,000-per-month consultant contracts for Ward 1. At the same meeting, her $90,000 real estate consulting contract collapsed when nobody could identify the funding source. That same evening, $1.4 million in consent agenda items was approved in 30 seconds.

Adding McKenzie's known $80,000-per-year Destination College Park tourism contract (voted on by the full council), the running total of Ward 1 spending linked to McKenzie's sponsorship or direction approaches $800,000 over roughly two years. This does not include the $330,000 STEM consulting contract flagged by residents on August 19, 2024, which was linked to a council appointee and slipped into a consent agenda with a conflict-of-interest flag.

The Vendors: Who Gets the Contracts

Several names recur across the McKenzie-linked spending.

Karen Jeremy runs Destination College Park, the tourism entity that received an $80,000 annual contract McKenzie approved. Jeremy also received a disputed $24,000 bonus payment that became the subject of a heated confrontation at the April 20, 2026 workshop session. When Mayor Bianca Motley Broom questioned whether Jeremy had met the three performance criteria required for the bonus, including exceeding local tourism targets by 10%, Jeremy deflected. She told the council the evaluation question should be directed to legal counsel, not to her. McKenzie responded by moving to override the mayor as chair of the meeting for what she characterized as "badgering" the contractor. Jeremy has appeared at multiple meetings defending her work and, on May 5, 2025, told council she was considering legal action over a social media smear campaign connected to her city contract. By November 2025, Jeremy told the council she was overseeing renovation contractors as a consultant.

Sabrina Willis, through RSB Services (also referenced as RSW Services), has received substantial city payments. Willis served as vice chair of BIDA and later became its chair. On February 3, 2025, resident Elizabeth Lester flagged that Willis's company was receiving city payments while Willis held a council-appointed position, raising conflict-of-interest concerns. By April 20, 2026, Lester returned with harder numbers. On June 2, 2025, McKenzie personally presented a glowing proclamation to Willis, weeks after a $68,000 Christmas party expense scandal at BIDA.

The $330,000 STEM consulting contract with Emergent Life Skills and Education Consulting, flagged on August 19, 2024, was linked by resident Lester to a council member's appointee. McKenzie later stated on the record that the organization had not yet had a contract executed or received payment, but the unsigned contract itself became a subject of dispute in November 2024.

The facade grant to "Jamelle McKenzie Brothers Realty" on May 5, 2025, was approved for $10,000. The shared surname raised questions, though this investigation has not confirmed a family relationship. It is important to note this gap.

The Rules: What Georgia Law and City Policy Require

Georgia law generally requires competitive sealed bids for public construction projects exceeding certain dollar thresholds. Professional services contracts, including consulting and engineering, often fall under exemptions that allow governments to select providers based on qualifications rather than lowest price. College Park's own procurement ordinance and charter establish additional guardrails, including spending limits for the city manager and requirements for council approval.

City Attorney Winston Denmark has, on other contracts, flagged procurement violations directly. In March 2026, Denmark blocked a $4.8 million roofing contract, citing Georgia bidding requirements for projects over $250,000 and noting that deferred maintenance does not qualify as an emergency. In May 2025, two agenda items were pulled after Denmark confirmed they violated procurement policy. In September 2025, Denmark flagged a tree removal contract where the agenda listed $100,000 but the actual contract showed $21,000.

What is less clear from the public record is whether Denmark or any procurement officer reviewed McKenzie's Ward 1 contracts with the same rigor. The $250,000 nature preserve engineering contract, the $250,000 train depot renovation, and the $42,000 trail improvement all passed without any on-record discussion of competitive bidding, vendor selection criteria, or procurement compliance. This does not prove the reviews did not happen at the staff level. It is possible that compliant paperwork exists but was simply never discussed in public meetings. However, the city's own procurement officer position has been vacant or in flux for extended periods. In September 2024, the interim procurement officer quit, leaving no one to even issue mandatory RFPs.

Denmark himself acknowledged the systemic problem at a September 2025 workshop, asking aloud whether the city was doing proper reviews when working under deadline pressure.

“Are we doing the proper reviews? Are we reviewing the contract with this vendor and going through all of our checks and protocols, uh, in an appropriate way, uh, to adequately protect the city when we're kind of working under the gun to get something done at the last minute.”City Attorney Winston Denmark
“Madame Mayor, I believe the city manager indicates that it's $100,000 and to the extent there's uh something different in the contract that you have in front of you, we'll if if the vote is to approve what's on the agenda, we'll conform the contract to your vote.”City Attorney Winston Denmark

The Defense: McKenzie's Case for Her Spending

McKenzie has been consistent in her defense, and her arguments deserve full airing.

On September 2, 2025, during the most direct confrontation over her contracting practices, McKenzie told the council that personal acquaintance with vendors is not only unavoidable but desirable. She also invoked her personal wealth as evidence that she has no corrupt motive. And she pointed to an existing precedent: the legislative assistant contracts that each council member holds, which were not competitively bid.

McKenzie has also framed the mayor's questioning of her contracts as a pattern of hostility toward Ward 1 vendors. At the April 20, 2026 workshop, she delivered an impassioned defense of Karen Jeremy, framing the mayor's bonus questions as an attack on a Black woman contractor.

On the $24,000 Jeremy bonus, McKenzie claimed sole authority over the decision, saying she alone decided to approve the payment based on her reading of the contract.

These arguments have some face validity. In a city of 15,000, it is genuinely difficult to find qualified vendors with no personal connection to elected officials. Professional services contracts are frequently exempt from competitive bidding under both state law and municipal procurement codes. And the legislative-assistant precedent is real: those contracts were not bid out either.

But the defense does not address the cumulative scale, which extends into the hundreds of thousands. It does not explain why a $90,000 real estate consulting contract was placed on the agenda without anyone being able to identify its funding source. And the argument that personal wealth precludes corruption is, on its own terms, not a procurement safeguard recognized by any Georgia ethics framework.

“If it weren't for me that I could not give a contract to somebody in this city cuz I know them personally, I would never be able to give a contract. And why do I select people to give contracts to that I know is because I can trust them.”Councilwoman Jamelle McKenzie, Ward 1 / Mayor Pro Tem
“We have professional service contracts with each of our legislative assistants. We did not bid those out. This is the same type of situation.”Councilwoman Jamelle McKenzie, Ward 1 / Mayor Pro Tem

The Comparison: Is McKenzie Uniquely Profligate, or Is the Problem Systemic?

A fair analysis requires asking whether McKenzie's spending stands apart from her colleagues or whether the problem runs across the entire council.

The evidence suggests it runs across the entire council.

On September 2, 2025, Councilman Roderick Gay's $625,000 parking lot repaving contract passed in the consent agenda without a word of discussion, alongside a ward transfer that put the land in Gay's ward. On September 15, 2025, a $242,000 paving contract sponsored by Gay for roads in his own Ward 4 was slipped into the consent agenda and passed with zero discussion. Gay added Winthrop Drive to an existing street contract via community enhancement funds on June 2, 2025, again with no separate vote.

By March 2, 2026, Gay articulated the cultural norm plainly.

Councilman Carn has his own pattern. On May 22, 2024, he created a consent agenda on the spot, sweeping 11 spending items into a single vote. A $50,000 Favor House contract, an $85,000 federal lobbyist renewal with no competitive bidding, and $70,000 legislative aide contracts have all moved through without competitive procurement.

The mayor has not been immune from criticism either. Residents have noted that her own community enhancement budget has gone unspent, and at least one contractor accused her of withholding $400,000 in capital campaign funds.

The point is not to excuse McKenzie by pointing to others. The point is that College Park may have a systemic procurement problem, not just a Ward 1 problem. When every council member directs six- and seven-figure spending through the consent agenda with no competitive bidding, the issue is structural.

“It ain't illegal to make your friends rich. It ain't against the law, believe it or not, to make your friends rich. If you do business with your friends, you just did business with your friends.”Council Member Roderick Gay

The Delivery Vehicle: How the Consent Agenda Makes It All Possible

Every contract detailed in this investigation shares one feature: the consent agenda. It is the procedural mechanism that transforms hundreds of thousands of dollars in first-time expenditures into routine business approved in seconds.

On October 6, 2025, Mayor Motley Broom tried to pull six consent agenda items for public discussion. No council member seconded her motion. It died without a vote. On April 20, 2026, Councilman Carn proposed a new policy requiring a council vote to pull any item from the consent agenda, further insulating bundled spending from scrutiny.

The consent agenda is designed for genuinely routine matters: approving minutes, ratifying previously discussed items, renewing standing contracts. In College Park, it has become something else entirely. On June 15, 2026, $1.4 million in spending across 10 items was approved without a single question. On September 2, 2025, over $1 million passed in approximately 15 seconds. On August 5, 2024, Carn created a consent agenda on the spot, mid-meeting, sweeping 11 spending items into a single uncontested vote that residents never saw coming.

The $42,000 trail improvement, the $250,000 nature preserve contract, the $250,000 train depot renovation, the $40,000 video content contract (initially), and the $10,000 facade grant all moved through this mechanism. Residents who want to object have to know the items exist, show up to the meeting, and speak during public comment, which occurs before the vote. Even then, their objections have no procedural weight.

Resident Elizabeth Lester and others have repeatedly flagged specific consent agenda items with detailed written and spoken public comments. The council has, in most cases, responded with silence.

“It has not gone unnoticed that the $40,000 video content contract was first buried in the consent agenda as if awarding tens of thousands of taxpayer dollars were the same as approving minutes and only now has it been moved to the regular agenda. Where it stands now

Council Watch has filed open records requests for all Ward 1 professional service contracts for fiscal years 2024 through 2026, community enhancement fund disbursements by ward, and any procurement waivers or sole-source justifications. If the city can produce compliant paperwork for the contracts identified in this investigation, that will be reported. Until then, the public record shows a pattern of spending that moved fast, moved quietly, and moved through channels designed to prevent questions.

The question James Walker posed on September 2, 2025 remains unanswered: "Has there been any indication that we've sought out other people besides the supporters of Jamelle McKenzie to get contracts in the city of College Park?" No council member responded that night. No council member has responded since.

The $90,000 real estate consulting contract that collapsed on June 15, 2026, may actually be the most instructive data point in this investigation, not because it was approved, but because it was not. Nobody could find the money. The system, for once, stopped itself. The question for College Park residents is why it took a missing funding source to halt a contract, rather than any of the safeguards that are supposed to come before that moment.
